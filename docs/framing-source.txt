# **Landscape Analysis, Scientific Positioning, and Experimental Design for Differentiable 3-Axis CNC Machining at RSS**

Framing a 5–6 page *Robotics: Science and Systems* (RSS) paper on a differentiable 3-axis CNC machining simulator requires bridging two historically separate research communities: **computer-aided manufacturing (CAM) geometry kernels**, which rely on exact but non-differentiable Boolean constructive solid geometry (CSG), dexel, or voxel subtractions1, and **robotics differentiable physics and reinforcement learning (RL)**, which study gradient propagation through discontinuous contact and material fracture5. Across the current academic landscape, prior work in subtractive manufacturing has optimized toolpaths using slow zero-order evolutionary algorithms over discrete simulators1, restricted optimization to static implicit surface fields that ignore time-dependent in-process workpiece (IPW) state1, or replaced the physical cutting kernel with a learned neural surrogate to approximate gradients1. Conversely, while the robotics community has developed differentiable simulators for soft-body knife cutting (DiSECt, TopoCut)7 and analyzed the bias-variance trade-off between first-order analytical smoothing and zero-order RL in rigid contact5, no prior work has formulated an analytical or volumetric differentiable simulator for rotary 3-axis CNC milling that directly compares smooth first-order trajectory optimization against zero-order RL over hard cutting across canonical CAM initializations (facing, contouring, and adaptive clearing).  
The central scientific insight of your RSS paper rests on the duality between **analytical smoothing (differentiable cutting)** and **randomized smoothing (RL over hard cutting)** when initialized from structured topological priors1. In 3-axis milling, exact material removal is governed by a discontinuous Heaviside step function at the cutter-workpiece boundary: a tool either engages stock or moves through air (yielding zero spatial gradient), and crossing a stepover boundary or internal corner induces a stiff discontinuity in Cutter-Workpiece Engagement (CWE) and cutting force1. Your simulator enables a controlled investigation of this optimization landscape. By relaxing hard Boolean subtraction into a smooth, differentiable material removal operator, first-order gradient descent can refine high-dimensional continuous trajectory coordinates and feed rates—equalizing CWE loads and smoothing curvature—in orders of magnitude fewer evaluations than RL1. However, because analytical gradients provide strictly local information16, uninitialized optimization collapses into poor local minima (such as air-cutting or incomplete pocket clearing), making topological initialization via facing, contouring, and adaptive templates essential1. Comparing smooth gradients against residual RL on hard cutting further clarifies where each paradigm excels: smooth differentiable operations dominate local continuous path deformation and force regulation, whereas zero-order RL better navigates discontinuous discrete transitions (such as stepover jumps or island avoidance) before encountering high variance during fine geometric convergence5.  
To support these claims within the 5–6 page RSS format, your experimental plan should center on a unified benchmark suite spanning three canonical 3-axis operations—**Facing** (direction-parallel raster passes), **Contouring** (boundary-offset profiles with sharp corners), and **Adaptive Clearing** (trochoidal constant-engagement roughing)—evaluated across four progressive experimental stages1. First, establish the **role of CAM initialization** by testing whether smooth gradient optimization and RL fail to clear stock from cold or random initializations due to vanishing air-cutting gradients and sparse rewards, whereas CAM-initialized trajectories converge reliably1. Second, execute a **head-to-head solver comparison** contrasting Smooth Differentiable Optimization (first-order Adam/L-BFGS with annealing and truncated horizons) against Hard Cutting via Residual RL (PPO/SAC) and zero-order evolutionary baselines, measuring cycle time, peak cutting force, CWE variance, volumetric IoU, overcut, and residual stock1. Third, conduct **mechanistic ablations** on the smoothing sharpness (![][image1]) and backpropagation horizon (![][image2]) to quantify gradient bias and variance against exact hard cutting1. Finally, close the loop with **physical 3-axis CNC machining experiments** (in machinable wax and Aluminum 6061\) to verify that smooth differentiable refinement reduces real-world cycle times and spindle force spikes without violating geometric tolerances1.

### **2.1 Proposed RSS Paper Abstract**

**Title:** Differentiable 3-Axis CNC Machining: Comparing Smooth Gradient Optimization and Hard-Cutting Reinforcement Learning from Canonical CAM Initializations  
**Abstract:**  
Generating physically optimal toolpaths for 3-axis Computer Numerical Control (CNC) machining requires reasoning over long-horizon trajectories where every motion permanently mutates the in-process workpiece (IPW) geometry and dictates subsequent cutter-workpiece engagement (CWE) forces. Historically, computer-aided manufacturing (CAM) has relied on rigid geometric heuristics—such as facing, contouring, and adaptive (trochoidal) clearing—because standard solid modeling kernels employ discontinuous Boolean subtractions that yield zero gradients during air-cutting and undefined derivatives at stock boundaries. While recent works have attempted to bypass this discontinuity using black-box evolutionary search, discrete-grid reinforcement learning (RL), or pre-trained neural surrogates, a fundamental question in physical AI remains unanswered: *how do first-order analytical gradients from a smoothly relaxed cutting simulator compare against zero-order RL operating on exact, hard Boolean cutting?* In this paper, we present a GPU-accelerated, dual-mode differentiable simulator for 3-axis CNC milling that supports both exact hard Boolean material removal and a continuously differentiable (smooth) volumetric cutting relaxation coupled with mechanistic cutting force and kinematic cycle-time models. Using this simulator, we conduct a systematic study of continuous trajectory optimization and residual RL across canonical CAM initializations (facing, contouring, and adaptive clearing). We demonstrate three core scientific insights: (1) because material removal gradients are strictly local, uninitialized trajectory optimization and RL both collapse into degenerate local minima, whereas initializing with topological CAM strategies enables reliable global convergence; (2) smooth differentiable cutting excels at low-variance, high-dimensional local refinement—smoothing trajectory curvature and equalizing peak CWE forces in orders of magnitude fewer iterations than RL; and (3) zero-order RL over hard cutting acts as implicit randomized smoothing across stiff stepover and corner discontinuities, discovering discrete pass modifications that smooth gradients miss, thereby motivating a hybrid warm-start paradigm. Physical 3-axis milling experiments validate that our simulator-optimized trajectories significantly reduce cycle time and peak spindle loads while maintaining tight dimensional tolerances.

### **2.2 Unique Scientific Merit and Insights Based on Differences with Prior Work**

Your paper makes four distinct scientific contributions that distinguish it from existing CAM, computer vision, and robotics literature.  
**Direct Analytical/Volumetric Differentiability vs. Black-Box Neural Surrogates.** The closest conceptual works in manufacturing—Lichter & Hadházi (2026)1 and Zhou et al. (2026) (CNCGEN)11—treat Boolean material removal as inherently non-differentiable and therefore replace the geometric subtraction kernel with a pre-trained neural network (![][image3] or a Surrogate Verifier) to predict CWE or volumetric loss. However, neural surrogates face out-of-distribution generalization risks when an optimizer pushes trajectories into unseen self-intersecting loops or thin residual walls, and they require costly offline training datasets1. By contrast, your simulator makes the **geometric and physical material removal operation itself differentiable** (via smooth spatial and depth relaxation of the cutter swept volume against the in-process stock representation)1. This yields physically consistent, zero-training-data gradients across arbitrary 3-axis geometries and tool radii.  
**Extending Differentiable Cutting from Soft-Tissue Slicing (DiSECt) to Rigid 3-Axis Rotary Milling.** In the RSS community, DiSECt (Heiden et al., 2021\)7 and TopoCut (Wang et al., 2025\)12 established differentiable simulation for cutting soft biomaterials with a thin knife blade along a planar cut. However, 3-axis CNC machining introduces fundamentally different physical and geometric mechanics: the tool is a rotating cylindrical, bull-nose, or ball-end mill that removes *volumetric swaths* rather than splitting a 2D manifold interface14; cutting forces depend on the instantaneous **Cutter-Workpiece Engagement (CWE) angle** (![][image14]) and undeformed chip thickness (![][image21]) integrated over the axial depth of cut (![][image22])14; and toolpaths are self-overlapping, multi-pass trajectories where Pass ![][image23] directly alters the stock boundary encountered by Pass ![][image24]1. Your simulator is the first to formulate this sequential, history-dependent rotary milling process within a differentiable computational graph.  
**Empirical Resolution of the "Smooth Analytical Gradients vs. Hard Zero-Order RL" Trade-Off in Subtractive Manufacturing.** Building on the theoretical framework of Suh et al. (ICML 2022\)5 and Pang et al. (T-RO 2023\)13, your paper characterizes how the Heaviside discontinuity of material removal governs solver performance in manufacturing. In hard Boolean cutting, if a toolpath segment lies slightly outside the stock (air-cutting), its spatial gradient with respect to material removal is identically zero (![][image25])13. Conversely, at the exact boundary of an internal corner or stepover, the CWE angle jumps discontinuously, causing first-order gradients to become unstable6. By introducing a smooth spatial relaxation (controlled by a sharpness hyperparameter ![][image1]), your simulator creates a continuous attraction/repulsion field around the stock boundary and target surface1, allowing first-order optimizers to smoothly redistribute control points, mitigate engagement spikes in corners, and maximize feed rates along low-curvature regions with low gradient variance1. Conversely, because zero-order RL (such as PPO or SAC) estimates policy gradients via stochastic action perturbations (![][image26]), it performs *randomized smoothing* over the discontinuous hard-cutting objective5. RL can therefore evaluate exact Boolean material removal without smoothing bias and discover discrete, non-local adjustments (such as shifting a pass across an island boundary or altering stepover overlap) where analytical gradients are flat or trapped13. However, because zero-order gradient variance scales inversely with perturbation variance and linearly with horizon length (![][image27])6, RL struggles to converge to the sub-millimeter geometric precision and C² kinematic smoothness required for CNC finishing1.  
**Topological Conditioning via Canonical CAM Initializations (Facing, Contouring, Adaptive).** Prior deep learning approaches such as CNC-Net attempt to synthesize toolpaths from scratch23, while DiffSkill (Lin et al., 2022\) demonstrated that uninitialized gradient descent in differentiable physics gets trapped in greedy local optima on multi-stage tasks16. Your paper formalizes **CAM-Initialized Trajectory Optimization and Residual RL**, showing that classical geometric strategies—**Facing** (zig-zag coverage), **Contouring** (offset boundary loops), and **Adaptive Clearing** (trochoidal loops)—act as topological homotopy classes1. Initializing within these classes provides global stock coverage while allowing smooth gradients or residual RL to solve the physical optimization problem that classical CAM templates ignore1.

### **3.2 Benchmark Workpiece Suite and CAM Initialization Protocols**

Design a standardized suite of **three 3-axis benchmark geometries** paired with the three canonical CAM initializations so that every method is tested across increasing geometric and physical complexity:

> 1. **Task 1: Facing on Asymmetric / Stepped Stock (Facing-Init):** This benchmark uses a non-rectangular (e.g., L-shaped or kidney-shaped) top stock surface with a variable-height step feature requiring planar facing to a target ![][image51] depth18. It is initialized with standard direction-parallel **Zig-Zag (Raster) Facing** (TP4 in standard CAM18) generated over the bounding box with a fixed stepover ![][image52] and sharp 90° turnaround corners. The primary optimization challenge is that bounding-box zig-zag passes spend substantial time air-cutting outside the non-rectangular stock boundary and suffer severe deceleration at sharp 90° stepover reversals1.  
> 2. **Task 2: Multi-Feature Pocket & Island Milling (Contour-Init):** This benchmark features a 2.5D/3D closed cavity containing sharp internal corners (60°–90°), a narrow bottleneck channel, and one or two isolated internal islands (modeled after the cavity benchmarks in Wan et al.17 and CNCGEN11). It is initialized with **Contour-Parallel Offset Loops** (Spiral-In / Spiral-Out or pairwise offset contours17) that follow the outer boundary and island perimeters. The optimization challenge arises because contour-parallel paths experience sudden spikes in CWE angle (![][image53] full slotting) when driving into sharp internal corners, leave uncut residual cusps in bottleneck regions, and exhibit sharp G¹ direction changes at offset-loop transitions1.  
> 3. **Task 3: Curved Variable-Width Slot & Deep Pocket Roughing (Adaptive-Init):** This benchmark models a curved, variable-width channel and deep pocket with a sloped or freeform floor requiring simultaneous 3-axis (![][image54]) motion1. It is initialized with a standard **Circular Trochoidal / Adaptive Clearing** trajectory (![][image55] superimposed along the slot medial axis)1. The optimization challenge is that true circular trochoids have excessively long total path lengths (spending roughly half of each revolution air-cutting during the return loop) and uneven engagement angles when slot width or floor depth varies14.

### **3.3 Compared Baselines and Algorithmic Architectures**

Across each of the three tasks, evaluate **six algorithmic conditions** parameterized over the exact same trajectory representation (a sequence of waypoints or cubic B-spline / NURBS control points ![][image56] and feedrate scalars ![][image57])1:

> 1. **Nominal CAM Heuristic (CAM-Base):** The unoptimized facing, contouring, or adaptive toolpath executed at constant nominal feedrate (or commercial CAM output from Fusion 360\)18.  
> 2. **Uninitialized Smooth Differentiable Optimization & Uninitialized RL (No-Init Diff-Opt & No-Init RL):** Initializing the trajectory from a straight line across the stock center or random control points (following the DiffSkill16 and CNC-Net11 ablation setups) to test whether local gradients or RL can synthesize valid 3-axis pocket clearing without a topological prior.  
> 3. **CAM-Initialized Zero-Order Black-Box Optimization (CAM \+ CEM / DE):** Optimizing spline control points on the **Hard Cutting** simulator using the Cross-Entropy Method (CEM), Differential Evolution (as in Jacsó et al.8), or MPPI (as in TopoCut12).  
> 4. **CAM-Initialized Hard Cutting via Residual RL (CAM \+ Hard-RL):** Formulating trajectory refinement as Residual RL (Johannink et al.54) where the action at control point or segment ![][image23] is a bounded spatial and feedrate residual ![][image58] added to the nominal CAM trajectory: ![][image59]54. Train using **PPO** and **SAC** on the **Hard Cutting** simulator, where state ![][image60] encodes a local downsampled Z-map or SDF patch around the tool, current cutter coordinates, nominal upcoming CAM waypoints, and instantaneous CWE force61.  
> 5. **CAM-Initialized Smooth Differentiable Optimization (CAM \+ Smooth-Diff):** Directly optimizing the control point residuals ![][image61] using first-order gradient descent (**Adam** or **L-BFGS**) backpropagated through the **Smooth Differentiable Simulator**1. Incorporate two stabilization mechanisms supported by prior work: (a) *Windowed / Truncated Horizon Backpropagation (![][image2] steps)*, detaching in-process stock state gradients every ![][image62] steps (inspired by SHAC/AHAC6) to prevent gradient explosion across hundreds of sequential passes; and (b) *Curriculum Annealing of Smoothing Sharpness (![][image1])*, starting with a wide smoothing kernel (![][image63] small) so air-cutting segments feel attraction toward uncut stock and sharp corner engagement gradients remain smooth, then annealing ![][image64] so the final optimization converges toward the exact hard-cutting geometry1.  
> 6. **Hybrid RL-Warmstarted Differentiable Optimization or First-Order Actor-Critic (CAM \+ Hybrid / AHAC):** Combining both paradigms by either running SHAC/AHAC using the smooth differentiable simulator for short-horizon policy gradients paired with a critic evaluated on hard cutting6, or running Hard-RL to discover discrete pass and stepover shifts followed by Smooth-Diff gradient descent to polish C² smoothness and eliminate residual stock.

### **3.5 Step-by-Step Experimental Roadmap (Mapped to Your 5–6 Page Paper Layout)**

The four core experiments below map directly to the claims in your abstract and fit within a 2–2.5 page RSS experimental section.

#### **Experiment 1: The Necessity of CAM Initialization (Escaping Local Minima in Long-Horizon Cutting)**

This experiment tests the hypothesis that because material removal gradients are spatially local (zero in air, non-zero only near the tool-stock interface), uninitialized optimization (No-Init Diff-Opt and No-Init RL) will become trapped in greedy local minima (leaving high residual stock or ploughing through islands), whereas initializing with Facing, Contouring, and Adaptive templates provides the global topological structure needed for convergence1. On the Multi-Feature Pocket (Task 2\) and Curved Slot (Task 3), compare No-Init Diff-Opt, No-Init RL, and CNC-Net-style uninitialized carving against CAM \+ Smooth-Diff and CAM \+ Hard-RL. The deliverable (Figure 2 and a subset of Table 1\) is a visual trajectory progression and convergence plot illustrating how uninitialized gradient descent greedily carves a local trench to the target depth and stalls (analogous to DiffSkill's greedy local-minimum analysis16), whereas CAM-initialized trajectories achieve high Volumetric IoU and minimal residual stock11.

#### **Experiment 2: Head-to-Head Comparison Across Facing, Contouring, and Adaptive Initializations**

This experiment tests the hypothesis that CAM \+ Smooth-Diff will outperform CAM \+ Hard-RL and CAM \+ DE/CEM by orders of magnitude in sample efficiency and wall-clock time while achieving superior cycle-time reduction, smoother curvature, and lower peak cutting forces, whereas CAM \+ Hard-RL will better discover discrete stepover or island-bypassing shifts when the initial CAM template contains topological redundancies1. Run all six methods across 5 random seeds on all three tasks (Facing-Init, Contour-Init, Adaptive-Init), recording learning curves against both simulation steps and GPU wall-clock time.  
Across the three strategies, analyze three mechanistic behaviors:

> 1. **In Facing (Task 1):** Test how Smooth-Diff pulls back control points that extend into empty air outside the asymmetric stock boundary and rounds 90° stepover turns into smooth C² teardrops to reduce cycle time, whereas Hard-RL reduces air-cutting via coarse step shifts but can introduce action-sampling jitter along raster lines1.  
> 2. **In Contouring (Task 2):** At sharp 90° internal pocket corners where CAM-Base encounters a severe cutting force spike, evaluate how Smooth-Diff uses the analytical CWE force gradient to bow the corner trajectory inward and schedule a local feedrate dip to cap ![][image48] below the safety threshold1. Conversely, test a pocket geometry where two islands create a redundant offset loop to show how Hard-RL (via stochastic exploration) can collapse or bridge a redundant pass while Smooth-Diff remains within its initial homotopy class13.  
> 3. **In Adaptive Clearing (Task 3):** Test whether Smooth-Diff automatically deforms circular trochoidal loops into compressed, asymmetric elliptical or B-spline loops directly from the physical objective—targeting the \~25–34% path length/time reductions and \~31% CWE variance reductions reported in specialized parametric studies by Zhou et al.14 and Jacsó et al.8—while evaluating whether Hard-RL struggles to coordinate the coupled ![][image70] phase of consecutive trochoidal loops.

#### **Experiment 3: Theoretical and Mechanistic Analysis of the Smooth-to-Hard Gap (Bias-Variance & Horizon Ablations)**

Following Suh et al.5 and Georgiev et al.6, this experiment tests the hypothesis that backpropagating through an entire multi-pass toolpath without horizon truncation (![][image71]) causes gradient instability due to compounding IPW state dependencies, while an overly stiff smoothing sharpness (![][image72]) causes high gradient variance at stepover boundaries and an overly soft ![][image73] introduces geometric overcut bias. Conduct two controlled sweeps: (1) a **Smoothing Sharpness (![][image1]) Sweep** evaluating single-step and multi-step gradient cosine similarity and variance across ![][image74] against zero-order Monte Carlo (Hard-RL) gradients (![][image75] samples, following AHAC6) as a cutter sweeps across a sharp stock stepover edge; and (2) a **Backpropagation Horizon (![][image2]) Sweep** optimizing Smooth-Diff and SHAC/AHAC across truncation horizons ![][image76]6. The deliverable (Figure 4\) is a two-panel diagnostic plot showing the U-shaped trade-off curve of smoothing bias versus gradient variance across ![][image1] (validating curriculum annealing) and final episodic performance versus truncation horizon ![][image2]6.

#### **Experiment 4: Physical 3-Axis CNC Milling Validation (Sim-to-Real Execution)**

This experiment tests whether trajectories optimized in your differentiable simulator transfer zero-shot to a physical 3-axis CNC milling machine, achieving the predicted reductions in cycle time and cutting force spikes while preserving dimensional part accuracy1. Follow the safe material progression established by Lichter & Hadházi1 and Chen & Kara21: execute physical cutting tests first on **machinable wax** (for safe geometric verification of all baselines including Hard-RL and No-Init) and then on **Aluminum 6061** on a 3-axis vertical CNC milling center (such as a Haas OM-2 or desktop/industrial 3-axis VMC with a solid carbide flat-end mill14). Export optimized trajectories as standard G-code for CAM-Base (Fusion 360), CAM \+ Hard-RL, CAM \+ Smooth-Diff, and CAM \+ Hybrid.
